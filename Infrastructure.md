## 1 Passenger & Cargo Transit: The Counterweighted Twin-Train System
 
Ships never transit a gate — the throat is sized for people and freight only, never hulls. Every torch-drive vessel ([Technology §1.1](Technology.md#1.1)) is built, fueled, and scrapped entirely within its home system; nothing built in one system can ever fly to another. This is a hard physical ceiling, not a policy — it holds equally before and after the severance, and it means Danu's post-independence propulsion shift (see Economy's Post-Collapse Resource Economics, [Economy §2](Economy.md#2)) was always a matter of developing local technical knowledge rather than replacing stranded Nova Reach hardware, since no such hardware could ever have crossed to begin with.
 
**Throat diameter: 2.5 m, network standard.** With hulls out of the equation, the governing constraint becomes the tightest single-body packing problem: one person, feet-first, aligned along the direction of travel, not seated crosswise as in a vehicle cabin. A capsule tube sized to bideltoid (shoulder) breadth plus margin for gear or a suit needs only ~0.9–1.1 m; two such tubes side by side, with a service gap between them for the static infrastructure (power, plumbing, fiber, sensors), plus a margin ring at the bore wall, sets the whole throat at 2.5 m. Capacity is never solved by widening the bore — throat area is the expensive dimension ([Cosmology §2.1](Cosmology.md#2.1) below) — it's solved by train length and batching instead.
 
**Zero-g boarding.** The gate anchor is non-rotating ([§2.1](#2.1)) and therefore permanently microgravity, so passengers never duck through a standing tunnel — they descend feet-first into the capsule and hold a bar for the crossing, the same posture a diver takes entering a narrow hatch. This is what makes the tight bore comfortable rather than claustrophobic: nothing about the passage requires headroom, walking clearance, or an aisle.
 
**The counterweighted pair.** Two matched trains cross the throat simultaneously, moving in mirrored fashion along a shared rail — the same principle as a funicular, where one car's mass counterbalances the other's rather than either needing to be small on its own. Before departure, the lighter of the two trains is loaded with ballast to bring both cars' masses as close to equal as engineering allows; cargo is used preferentially as that ballast whenever a shipment's mass is convenient for trimming, with inert ballast blocks making up whatever remainder cargo can't cover. Because the mouth only ever sees the residual imbalance between the pair rather than either train's full mass, this keeps a two-second crossing from ever threatening the threading field the way a single unbalanced transit would ([Cosmology §2](Cosmology.md#2), [Cosmology §5](Cosmology.md#5)).
 
Wormhole engineers monitor that residual live during every crossing and trim the fermion/flux supply to absorb whatever small mismatch remains — the same control loop already described above, now watching a balance signal instead of a single mass swing. Departures are run as discrete, scheduled events rather than continuous flow: a matched pair boards, is weighed and trimmed, and crosses together, then the chamber resets for the next pair. Synchronization between the two cars — position, speed, and timing — is the scheme's single point of failure; a stall or desync on either side turns a balanced pair back into the unbalanced single-sided mass swing the whole design exists to avoid.
 
**Exact cancellation, not just risk reduction.** Given the permanent-transfer physics above ([Cosmology §2](Cosmology.md#2)), a simultaneous bidirectional pair does more than soften a single crossing's danger — a perfectly mass-matched pair cancels the long-term drift completely: whatever mass mouth A gains from the inbound train, it loses an equal amount to the outbound train, and the same holds at mouth B. This is the real, complete fix for chronic mass drift between connected systems, not merely a per-crossing safety margin.
 
**Asteroid regolith as the ballast source.** Farfield's preference for siting gates on asteroids and rubble-pile bodies ([§2.1](#2.1)) is doubly justified: beyond such bodies being the only class of host that guarantees an undifferentiated, negligible-gravity, usable throat location, they also supply the ballast itself. Loose rock and regolith, quarried directly from the host body, trims the lighter train to match the heavier one — no supply chain, no opportunity cost, just local material. Over Sat-48's 65+ year operating history this amounts to a real, visible quarrying operation on the host moon's surface, though nowhere near enough net volume to meaningfully deplete or destabilize the body itself.
 
**The residual has to be unbiased, not just small.** A small leftover mismatch on any single crossing is safe in isolation, but per the permanent-transfer physics, a *systematically biased* residual — a scale reading consistently light, say — accumulates every single crossing, walking the wormhole's mass steadily off its tuned target over months or years even though no individual crossing ever looked dangerous. This is a slow, boring, easy-to-miss failure mode distinct from a single catastrophic overshoot: the kind of thing that only surfaces when someone finally audits a gate's mass against its logged baseline and finds it's wandered.
 
**What the crossing actually feels like.** Despite the throat's density ([§2.1](#2.1)), far-field gravity at a gate mouth is negligible — nowhere near black-hole compactness — so there's no dramatic lensing or pull to notice, and given the bore's short proper length, the physical passage is over in a couple of seconds. What passengers do notice is the threading flux itself: an ozone-sharp ionization smell, hair lifting and a faint static bite off exposed metal, a brief actinic flash at the threshold, and a low subsonic hum from the injector machinery underfoot. The more disorienting part of the trip is mundane and brackets the crossing rather than being part of it — the shift from the rotating habitat's 0.5g, through the gate anchor's zero-g, through the capsule, and back to gravity on the far side. First-time travelers commonly need a beat to metabolize that there was no lived interval between two points that may be tens of millions of light-years apart; the low-grade disorientation that follows has its own settled slang, **"gate-lag"** — understood colonist-wide to be psychological, not physical, since there's no clock-shift at all to account for it.

# 2. The Sat-# Station Naming Convention
 
Wormhole gate stations are numbered sequentially across the founding corporation's entire build history — the number reveals how many gates the corporation had fully expanded (Stage 3) before this one. Other corporations maintain their own separate numbering conventions.
 
A station is one continuous physical structure that threads through every wormhole mouth it's built around, sharing power, water, comms, air, and pedestrian/cargo access across all connected systems — conceptually identical to Terminals A/B/C all being part of one airport. The whole structure carries one Sat-# designation regardless of how many systems it spans; individual system segments take a locational qualifier (e.g. "Sat-48 Nova Reach"). See [§2.1](#2.1) for the physical architecture of a Safford wing.
 
Colloquial names drift from the Sat-# over generations of casual use, the way real place names erode (cf. Dubh Linn → Dublin).

## 2.1 Station Architecture: Anchors, Bridge, and Habitat Tiers
 
Every Safford wing — Nova Reach, Danu, Obatala, and Enlil alike — is built on the same basic plan, driven by three constraints that all point the same direction: the gate throat needs a fixed, non-rotating, precisely stable mount ([Cosmology §2](Cosmology.md#2)); the resident population needs rotational gravity to remain healthy over a permanent, multi-generational lifespan; and the host body (dwarf moon, minor moon, belt object, or rubble-pile remnant) has negligible gravity of its own, so nothing can simply rest on a foundation — every structure must be mechanically anchored in.
 
### Two-anchor, bridge-spanned layout
 
Rather than a single structure cantilevered off one anchor point, each wing is built across two separate anchor clusters driven into the host body, joined by a rigid, vibration-damped bridge. This spans loads between two piers rather than stacking them all on one line, keeping the gate end mechanically quiet regardless of what the habitat end is doing.
 
- **Gate anchor:** hosts the gate chamber(s), tuning and control systems, and docking. At Nova Reach's wing, three individually sealed, independently tunable gate chambers are clustered here — one per branch mouth (Danu, Obatala, Enlil) — so a failure in one chamber cannot propagate to the others, the same risk-quarantine logic already applied at the network scale between Sat-39 and Sat-48 ([§3](#3)). The other three wings host a single gate chamber, connecting back to Nova Reach.
- **Habitat anchor:** a second, separate pier cluster into the same body, positioned to absorb the rotating habitat's torque and vibration locally rather than transmitting it back down a shared structure to the gate.
- **Bridge:** the rigid truss spanning the two anchors, carrying pedestrian and cargo transit and the shared power, water, air, and comms lines ([§2](#2)). Flexibly jointed at both ends so it doesn't become a resonance path between the two anchors.
On Enlil's loose rubble-pile site, a concentrated point-load anchor isn't viable, so both anchor clusters are built as a wide-footprint "raft" of piers spread across a stabilized patch of the body rather than a few deep piers — a structural reason (on top of the wing's small population and low investment) that Enlil's Safford wing reads as more improvised and skeletal than the other three.
 
### Gate chamber internals
 
A gate chamber is a squat, heavily braced installation, not an open archway — most of its visible bulk is the machinery holding the throat's mass/charge/flux balance in place, not open passage space.
 
- **Fermion injector ring:** an accelerator structure generating coherent, phase-matched fermion pairs and firing them at the throat under precise timing control — the largest single component, since coherent coupling ([Cosmology §2.3](Cosmology.md#2.3)) is the hardest part of the job.
- **Flux-shaping electrode cage:** a surrounding array of charged electrodes/coils sustaining the threading field, tuned continuously rather than pulsed, functionally similar in spirit to a tokamak's confinement coils.
- **Anchor bracing:** given the throat's density even at rest — on the order of a million kg/m³, hundreds of times denser than lead, packed into a couple of meters — the chamber is braced directly into the gate anchor's piers (above) so that concentrated mass never transmits stress into the rest of the station.
- **Control room:** wormhole engineers ([Culture §3](Culture.md#3)) read live Qe/M telemetry and coupling-efficiency data here, making the fine corrections automated systems can't — this is the actual content of their expertise, not day-to-day operation.
In steady, correctly-tuned operation the chamber is radiologically quiet: there's no black hole yet, so no Hawking radiation, and the danger is a sudden terminal event if the balance fails, not ambient exposure. Hazard/maintenance crews ([Culture §3](Culture.md#3)) do the physical EVA work on the injector ring, electrode cage, and anchor structure itself — the routine unglamorous labor that puts them closest to the machinery if something does go wrong.
 
### Emergency response, not distance shielding
 
Farfield does not build in extra bridge length as protection against a gate-mouth failure. Per [Cosmology §2.5](Cosmology.md#2.5), a collapsing mouth is already an existential event for the wing's gate chamber, docking traffic, and trade route regardless of how far away the habitat sits — there is no design distance that meaningfully mitigates that outcome, so the company doesn't spend money chasing one. The terminal gamma burst is instead handled as an emergency-response problem: radiation alarms, evacuation protocol, and hazard-crew procedure, consistent with Farfield's broader pattern of amoral cost efficiency ([Politics §3](Politics.md#3)).
 
### Habitat tiers, scaled to population
 
Rotational gravity is standard across all four wings — a genuinely permanent population living on the stations themselves (as at Nova Reach, [§5](#5)) cannot rely on exercise regimens alone over a multi-generational lifespan. The network targets a partial-gravity standard of roughly 0.5g, a deliberate cost/mass tradeoff against a full 1g field; the long-term consequence is a colonist population that has never experienced full Earth gravity.

**That consequence is real and stable, not merely theoretical.** Bone density and muscle mass adapt to mechanical loading (Wolff's Law), and loading scales directly with body weight, which scales with local g — a body receiving half the compressive strain of Earth gravity every day, for a lifetime, has no physical stimulus to maintain full Earth-normal bone architecture. No real long-duration human data exists at exactly 0.5g, but the underlying mechanism is well-characterized enough to predict the outcome with real confidence: multi-generational Nova Reach natives settle into a genuinely different, but **stable and healthy**, physiological baseline — measurably lower bone density and muscle mass than an Earth-raised or Lugh-raised person, fully load-bearing and unremarkable for a life actually lived at 0.5g. This is categorically different from Boann's case ([§3](#3), Boann subsection), where the stimulus is so far below maintenance threshold that loss is continuous and pathological rather than settling into equilibrium. The nearest real-world analogue is chronic high-altitude adaptation (Andean or Himalayan populations' elevated red blood cell count and lung volume) — a genuine, stable, healthy divergence from the species baseline, not an illness. The difference only becomes a practical problem when a Safford-born body meets a *higher*-g environment than it's built for: Earth itself, or even Lugh's comparatively modest 0.70g — a smaller, subtler echo of the return problem Boann-return workers face, worth remembering for any Safford-born character traveling somewhere heavier than home.
 
Habitat structure at the habitat anchor scales in three tiers as a wing's population grows:
 
- **Branch wings — short arc-module.** Danu, Obatala, and Enlil's Safford wings are working gate terminals only: docking, hazard/maintenance crews for the gate chamber, capsule loading, life support, and minimal medical/food service for the on-site rotational crew. There is no customs function here — this is internal Farfield territory pre-collapse, not a border crossing — and no civilian or family housing, since crew serve rotational tours rather than settling. Each uses the same short-arc-module architecture as Nova Reach's own founding structure (Year 0 subsection below): a partial-arc torus segment on the network-standard 111.8 m/2.000 rpm/0.5g design, balanced by the same four-mass counterweight system (habitat plus co-rotating balance weight; counter-rotating pair of ballast weights), scaled to each wing's actual crew size:

  | Wing | Crew | Arc length | Arc angle |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Enlil | 50 | 22.0 m | 11.3° |
  | Obatala | 90 | 39.7 m | 20.3° |
  | Danu | 150 | 66.1 m | 33.9° |

  This crew are Nova Reach citizens and Farfield personnel, not local colonists (see "Safford is Nova Reach territory," [Politics §4.1](Politics.md#4.1)) — they are not counted toward any colony's own population figures (Lugh/Boann, Yemoja/Olokun, Ereshkigal), and nobody commutes to Safford from the surface; crew live on-station for the duration of their rotational tour.
- **Large — multiple independent structures.** Nova Reach's wing (≈25,000–35,000 settled, ~39,000+ built capacity), hosting the bulk of the network's population and all three branch mouths, is the only wing to grow past this tier — via a sequence of independently-built, counter-rotating structures added over Sat-48's history rather than a single design executed once. See "Year 0: The Founding Arc-Module" and "The Growth Sequence: Rings A–D" below for the actual mechanism and numbers.
Because Sat-48 has operated for 65+ years, Nova Reach's wing shows visible layered evidence of its own growth — Ring A's original small quad still standing near its much larger later additions — while the three branch wings, never having grown past their initial operational-crew requirements, still look close to how they were first built.
 
### Deck Pitch and the Healthy Gravity Range

Two reference standards used throughout this section and elsewhere in the bible (including the Year 0 arc-module design below):

**Deck pitch: 4.5 m total** — 4 m clear headroom plus 0.5 m for structural floor plate and the service void (power, water, air, data) beneath it. This is the standard unit of radial spacing between decks anywhere radial stacking is used.

**Floor area per resident: 40 m²** — a mid-range planning figure (no dedicated on-station agriculture needed, since Trelium and Goibniu extraction are the wing's economic purpose, not food production), used to convert ring floor area into population capacity below.

**Healthy gravity range**, informed by real long-duration microgravity data (0g is unambiguously harmful per ISS-era medicine), with the upper bound set by Coriolis/vestibular comfort rather than any known injury threshold. This is Farfield's own official medical guidance for *designed* habitats — Safford's rings are engineered to it. Boann's actual, lived population does not observe it (see "Boann: World and Settlement" below) — not because the guidance is wrong, but because Farfield's own incentive structure quietly overrides it for a subset of Boann's workforce.

| Zone | Range | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Fully healthy, indefinite | 0.4g – 1.0g | Standard permanent housing |
| Marginal, medically monitored | 0.2g – 0.4g (lower) / 1.0g – 1.5g (upper) | Not standard housing; requires monitoring |
| Officially rotational-only | < 0.2g | Mandatory rotation out, per Farfield's own stated policy — not what actually happens on Boann |
| Trained/occupational only | > 1.5g | Never permanent residential |

The partial-g thresholds (as opposed to 0g's harm, which is well-established) reflect Farfield's best available in-universe medicine rather than confirmed real-world science — a corporate judgment call, not a hard physical limit.

### Year 0: The Founding Arc-Module

Nova Reach wing's very first habitat structure had to solve a problem distinct from anything the tier framework above addresses: house a small, specialized founding crew (roughly 100–150 people — the wormhole engineers, technicians, and support staff needed to characterize and inflate the branch seeds, per [Cosmology §2.2](Cosmology.md#2.2) and [Culture §3](Culture.md#3)) as cheaply and simply as possible, with no population history yet to justify a full ring. The design that resulted became Nova Reach wing's permanent design basis rather than a discarded prototype, and is preserved here in full engineering detail rather than smoothed over into a simpler abstraction.

**Why not a full ring.** A complete 360° torus at any reasonable width vastly overshoots what ~100–150 people need: even a modest 30 m-wide deck at rim radius produces on the order of 22,000+ m² of floor, more than ten times the founding crew's actual functional requirement (roughly 1,450 m², built up from lab/characterization space, control room, living quarters, galley, medical, exercise, mechanical/utility space, and general storage — see working breakdown below). Building a full ring for this population would mean paying for circumference nobody needs. The founding structure instead uses only a short **arc segment** of what would eventually become a full ring — a bent partial torus, not a complete one.

**Gravity target and rotation rate.** Using the same real-physics reasoning that governs every rotating structure in the network (a = ω²r), Year 0's engineers set 0.5g at the rim using a clean 2.000 rpm, giving a major radius of **111.8 m**. This figure — not 120 m — is Nova Reach wing's actual founding design radius, and it holds for every structure built at Sat-48 afterward (see "The Growth Sequence: Rings A–D" below): Farfield never re-derives or standardizes to a different radius partway through the wing's history. (Other wings' medium- and small-tier rings, built independently and much later, are not bound to this same figure.)

**Shape: a bent circular tube, not a stadium hull.** A single flat deck at 111.8 m radius, sized to the crew's actual floor-area need, would require an axial width of under 2 m — absurdly narrow. The alternative of stacking multiple flat decks *radially* solves the area problem, but raised a separate question: does the tube's cross-section need to be a true circle, a flattened/stadium hull, or something else? Because the arc segment genuinely bends (see below), a stadium hull's flat panels would have to be built as faceted, seamed segments to follow that curve — a structural weak point. A true circular cross-section has no such problem: it is, in effect, a short **partial torus** ("banana module"), carrying pure hoop stress continuously along its whole bent length exactly as the network's full rings already do ([§2.1](#2.1) above), with no distinction between "straight" and "bent" sections of the shell.

**Deck stacking and module sizing.** Decks stack radially outward from a low-gravity anchor at 111.8 m (0.5g), tapering in usable width like chords of the tube's circular cross-section — narrow near the tube's inner and outer walls, widest at its center — using the network-wide deck pitch standard (4.5 m: 4 m clear height + 0.5 m structure/utility void). For a given deck count *n*, minor radius, per-deck gravity, and the arc length needed to hit the 1,450 m² target all follow mechanically; the one genuine design choice is how many decks to stack. Fewer decks demand an absurdly long, ribbon-thin arc (a single deck needs a ~322 m arc, wrapping most of the way around the ring); many decks make the arc almost nonexistent but the cross-section enormous. The **closest-to-square** module — arc length nearest to the cross-section's own diameter, maximizing usable floor shape rather than a long narrow strip — lands at **5 decks**:

| Deck | Radius | Gravity | Chord width | Floor area |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 114.05 m | 0.510g | 13.50 m | 215.7 m² |
| 1 | 118.55 m | 0.530g | 20.62 m | 329.5 m² |
| 2 | 123.05 m | 0.550g | 22.50 m | 359.5 m² |
| 3 | 127.55 m | 0.570g | 20.62 m | 329.5 m² |
| 4 | 132.05 m | 0.591g | 13.50 m | 215.7 m² |

Minor (cross-section) radius: **11.25 m**. Arc length: **~16.0 m**. Total floor area: **~1,450 m²**, matching the founding crew's functional requirement almost exactly. Gravity ranges 0.510g–0.591g across the module — a real, deliberate gradient (quarters and administration toward the outer, higher-g decks; labs and less gravity-sensitive work toward the inner decks), comfortably within the 0.5g–1.0g band the module was designed against, well clear of either edge.

**Angular momentum and dynamic balance.** A single arc module with no counter-rotating partner creates two distinct structural problems, not one. First, its bulk rotation constitutes real angular momentum that, left uncancelled, applies a steady reaction torque against the gate anchor — a problem with teeth here specifically because the gate anchor's whole function depends on staying precisely fixed and non-rotating ([§2.1](#2.1) above). Second, and separately, a mass sitting off the spin axis sweeps its own weight around in a circle as the structure turns, loading the bearings with an oscillating side-force exactly like an unbalanced washing machine drum — dynamic imbalance, distinct from the angular-momentum problem and requiring its own fix.

Both are solved by the same simple structure, because the module's actual mass distribution (spread across its 5 decks, radius 114–132 m) turns out to have a radius of gyration of 123.185 m — within 0.11% of its simple geometric center (123.05 m). This tight spread means the whole module can be treated, for balance purposes, as an idealized point mass **M** at radius **R ≈ 123 m**, and from there the fix is genuinely elegant:

- **Co-rotating assembly** (shares the hab module's own bearing, spins with it): the hab module itself (mass M) plus one **balance weight** (mass M), diametrically opposite.
- **Counter-rotating assembly** (independent bearing, spins the opposite direction at the same rate): two more weights (mass M each), diametrically opposite each other.

Each assembly is individually balanced (equal mass on opposite sides of the axis — no wobble), and the two assemblies' angular momenta are equal and opposite (net zero reaction torque on the gate anchor) — four masses total, all identical, all at the same radius, with no new numbers introduced beyond what the hab module itself already established. A compact high-speed flywheel alternative was examined and rejected: even a generously-sized 2,000 t flywheel at 10 m radius would need a rim speed (~460 m/s) already pushing steel's material limits, and smaller flywheels need speeds many multiples beyond any known material — the angular momentum involved is simply too large to park in a small fast wheel. Matching mass at matching radius, spun the opposite direction, is the only approach that stays within real material limits.

The three non-habitat masses are inert ballast — no life support, no decking, no pressure hull beyond a containment shell — quarried directly from the host moon using the same regolith-ballast operation already established for gate-crossing trim mass ([§1](#1)). Comparable total tonnage to the habitat module itself, but overwhelmingly cheap tonnage, since it's local rock rather than built structure.

**The expansion pathway this opens.** Because the balance math only cares about mass and radius, not what the mass *is*, Farfield can scale the founding station by simply replacing ballast with more habitat: swap all three counterweights for habitat modules of matching mass, and population capacity roughly quadruples with no new balance engineering at all — the two co-rotating masses still balance each other, the two counter-rotating masses still balance each other, and the counter-rotating pair's angular momentum still exactly cancels the co-rotating pair's. Two of the four modules spin "the other way," which is imperceptible to their residents, since centrifugal gravity depends only on ω² and r, not rotation direction.

Beyond that 4x step, three further strategies exist, with different trade-offs:
- **Filling out the arc** — lengthening all four existing modules together. Structurally the trickiest option, since it requires coordinated, simultaneous growth of all four masses to keep the running balance intact throughout construction, not an independent add to any one of them.
- **New independent balanced "quads"** — a fresh set of four M/R masses added elsewhere on the shared spine, each internally balanced and never touching the existing structure's trim. This is the cleanest expansion path, and mirrors the existing rule that new rings are always added as matched counter-rotating pairs ([§2.1](#2.1) above) — just doubled, into matched pairs of matched pairs.
- **Graduating to full rings** — once population genuinely justifies it, this is the limiting case of an arc module as its angular span grows toward a full 2π: at that point a dedicated counterweight is no longer needed at all, because the ring balances directly against its own counter-rotating twin the way Nova Reach's mature rings already do ([§2.1](#2.1) above), and the arc-module design converges naturally with the Tier 1/2/3 architecture already described.

**Continuous fine trim.** Independent of the coarse four-mass structure, ballast position can be adjusted radially in real time via cable-and-winch (pulley) systems — hauling mass outward or inward to correct for population drift, cargo load changes, or wear, without ever touching the underlying M/R balance the station is built around. This mirrors the same two-tier control philosophy the wormhole gates themselves already use: a coarse structure fixed by design, with a continuous live-trim loop layered on top ([§1](#1)'s fermion/flux trimming against the residual mass mismatch) — evidently a recurring instinct in Farfield's engineering culture, not something unique to the gates.

*Note on areal mass density: the tonnage figures above (module mass, ballast tonnage) depend on an areal structural-mass density (kg per m² of floor) that has not yet been locked as canon — see Open Questions.*

### The Growth Sequence: Rings A–D

The Year 0 arc-module design above is a starting point, not an endpoint — over Sat-48's history, Farfield scales the wing through four distinct, independently-built structures, each holding to the same 111.8 m/2.000 rpm/0.5g-anchor design established at Year 0. Each is its own physically separate assembly with its own bearings, not an enlargement of what came before.

**Ring A — the original quad.** The four Year 0 arc modules (§ above — one habitat, one co-rotating balance weight, two counter-rotating weights, all swapped to matching habitat mass per the 4x expansion logic) are extended in lockstep from their original ~8.2° arc out to 45° each, keeping all four masses matched throughout construction to preserve balance at every step. Population: **877**.

**Ring B — first new ring pair.** An entirely new, independently-bearinged structure elsewhere on the shared spine, using the same 5-deck cross-section (minor radius 11.25 m) as Ring A, but grown to a full 360° ring rather than a partial arc. Initially balanced against a plain ballast counter-ring (unhabited, quarried mass, matching radius and mass); once justified, that ballast ring is swapped for a second habitable ring, giving two full counter-rotating rings that balance directly against each other with no ballast needed at all. Population: **3,508**.

**Ring C — second new ring pair, larger cross-section.** A second all-new independent structure, same 111.8 m anchor radius and design tradition, but stacked to **20 decks** (minor radius 45 m, spanning 111.8–201.8 m, outermost-deck gravity 0.892g) rather than 5. Built the same way as Ring B — ballast counter-ring first, later swapped for a second habitable ring. Population: **34,940**.

Cumulative built capacity across Rings A–C: **39,325** — comfortably covering, and somewhat exceeding, Nova Reach's established Year 92 population of 25,000–35,000 (§ Chronology). The gap is deliberate: Farfield builds ahead of settled population, not to it, and the ~4,000–14,000-person surplus of finished-but-under-occupied capacity is simply capacity waiting for growth that was still expected to continue.

**Farfield's standing engineering philosophy, made explicit here:** gravity varies meaningfully within a single structure (0.510g–0.591g in Ring A's decks, 0.500g–0.892g across Ring C's 20 decks) and this is treated as an acceptable, unremarkable cost of construction speed and cross-section economy, not a defect to be engineered around. "People will adjust" is the operative institutional attitude — fully consistent with, and a direct architectural expression of, Farfield's already-established amoral efficiency ([Politics §3](Politics.md#3)): safety and structural soundness are non-negotiable, comfort is not.

**Ring D — the unfinished fourth ring.** Construction begins on a fourth independent structure, again at the 111.8 m anchor, planned to push the radial-stacking design to its absolute physical ceiling: **25 decks** (minor radius 56.25 m, spanning 111.8–223.55 m) — the maximum depth obtainable before the outermost deck would cross 1.0g. If completed as a full counter-rotating ring pair, Ring D alone would have added roughly **116,900** further capacity, dwarfing Rings A–C combined and pushing the wing's total built capacity well past 150,000.

It is never finished. The Severance Event (Year 92, [Cosmology §2.5](Cosmology.md#2.5)) halts construction outright, and Ring D is left as an incomplete skeleton — partial ring segments, exposed structural framework, exactly the kind of half-built ambition that reads as a physical monument to the future Farfield still expected right up until the collapse. Its final planned specifications (exact deck count reached before halting, whether a counter-ring had even been started) are an open dramatic question rather than fixed canon — useful as a setting for a scene, and a visible, permanent reminder to post-collapse Nova Reach of how confidently the company was still expanding the day everything changed.

### Deck Numbering Convention

Every ring numbers its decks ascending outward from the hub, following the precedent already set by the Year 0 module's own Deck 0–4 table (above): **low numbers sit near the spin axis (lower gravity), high numbers sit at the rim (higher gravity)**. This holds network-wide rather than varying ring to ring, so residents and visitors never have to relearn the scheme moving between rings. Colloquially, people speak of going "up" toward the hub and "down" toward the rim — matching ordinary gravity intuition even though it inverts a skyscraper's usual up/down sense.

### Ring C: Deck Layout, Spoke Access, and the Retail Concourse

Ring C's 20 decks (minor radius 45 m, spanning r=111.8–201.8 m, per "The Growth Sequence: Rings A–D" above) divide into functional bands, derived from the same chord-tapering geometry established at Year 0 (floor width narrowest near the tube's inner/outer walls, widest at the cross-section's center):

| 4–9 | 127–150 m / 0.57–0.69g | 68–89 m (widening) | General housing — family apartments, communal courtyards, small shops bleeding in as the deck widens. |

**Spoke access.** Ring C uses **6 radial spokes**, spaced 60° apart around the ~940 m concourse loop (~120 m walk between adjacent spoke junctions) — multiple spokes both for structural load distribution around a ring this large and because the counter-rotating pair architecture (per "Year 0: The Founding Arc-Module" above) requires spoke connections at the shared hub on both co-rotating rings, not a single line.

- **Rim end (Deck 1, r≈114 m, 0.51g):** each spoke's outer terminus lands at Deck 1, the innermost/lowest-gravity deck and thus the shortest possible spoke run. A **secondary local lift** then runs radially within the tube's own 45 m cross-section, stopping at every deck (2 through 20) — the actual "vertical" hop that reaches the concourse (10–11) or the outer housing bands (18–20). Farfield doesn't fund an express spoke straight to retail; reaching the concourse always means rim spoke, then local lift.
- **Hub end (r≈0, zero-g):** the spoke car crosses from the rotating ring to the non-rotating hub via a **rotary bearing/slip-joint** — a sealed, continuously-turning coupling passing structural load, power, air, and pressure across the rotating/non-rotating boundary, the same category of engineering problem as a despun antenna platform or a large space station's solar-array rotary joints. The car's own spin component decelerates smoothly as it approaches the axis (less circumference to cover per rotation near r=0), so passengers experience the transition as sideways sway simply running out, not a hard mechanical handoff. Coriolis sway peaks around mid-radius and fades to nothing at the hub — noticeable to a first-time rider.

Ring A, built to the much smaller 4-module Year 0 quad, uses **4 spokes** instead — one per original mass — rather than the arbitrary even spacing above; same rim/hub mechanics, smaller scale.

### Ring Eras: Decor and Condition (proposed, not yet dated to exact years)

Each ring's build era is visible in its fabric and social texture. Exact construction years beyond Ring A (Year 0) and Ring D (halted Year 92) aren't locked — flagged here as design guidance rather than fixed canon:

- **Ring A — oldest.** Built when the venture was still unproven and everything shipped from Earth (pre-local Trelium production, [§4](#4)). Cramped (11.25 m minor radius), ISS-era industrial: visible conduits, riveted panels, analog gauges. Administrators relocated out once Ring B, then Ring C, came online, so Ring A's original hub-adjacent offices and quarters are now repurposed — archives, low-priority offices, an unofficial "founders' wing" with genuine, unmanufactured historical weight and the network's heaviest cumulative patch-and-retrofit history.
- **Ring B — first full ring.** Marks the shift from ad hoc arc-module construction to a proper 360° ring — the moment the wing stopped being an outpost and became a settlement. Functional corporate modernism, first standardized signage/wayfinding. By Year 92 it carries decades of lived-in accumulation: unofficial murals, worn communal courtyards, the "everybody knows everybody's business" texture of [§2.2](#2.2). Working-class, characterful, unglamorous.
- **Ring C — newest completed, "shiny."** Largest cross-section (45 m minor radius), best available engineering knowledge, widest gravity range. This is where administrators moved for the upgrade, including the Director of Transportation's office at the hub — bright, uniform, mass-produced fit-out, genuinely nicer but with none of Ring A/B's accumulated history yet. A real quality gradient runs outward: showroom-grade finish near the hub/admin decks, progressively cheaper finish moving out toward the hazard-crew decks (18–20) — Farfield's cost-efficiency philosophy made physically visible in the walls themselves.
- **Ring D — halted mid-construction, Year 92.** Bare structural skeleton — exposed girders, unpressurized sections, work lights and tools abandoned exactly where crews dropped them days before the collapse. No interior fit-out at all. A frozen monument to Farfield's confidence right up to the moment everything stopped; usable later as a salvage site, hideout, or a gut-punch view from a Ring C viewport.

## 2.2 Safford as Company Town: Daily Life and Education

Safford (specifically Nova Reach's wing, by far the network's largest) is best understood not as a military base or a transient work camp, but as a **company town** in the real historical sense — closer to Pullman, Illinois than to a naval station. People don't rotate through; they live and die there, raise children there, and by Year 92 most residents are third- or fourth-generation, with no lived memory of Earth or of a time before Farfield's total control. The distinction matters: a military base has hierarchy and discipline as its point; a company town has hierarchy and discipline as the *cost* of a place people actually call home.

### The recruitment pitch and its legacy

Farfield's original colonist recruitment (first- and second-wave, [Culture §1](Culture.md#1)) was built on a paternalistic promise that still structures the station's social contract two generations later: bring your family, and Farfield provides housing, food, and education, and guarantees employment once your children come of age. This is the same logic that built Pullman's houses and schools — not generosity for its own sake, but a durable way to secure a stable, controlled workforce that has nowhere else to go and no reason to leave. Sat-48's total isolation (no gate leads anywhere but through Nova Reach, and no ship built in-system can ever cross a throat, [§1](#1)) makes this promise unusually absolute: there is no rival employer, no next town over, no possibility of emigrating for a better offer. That absence of an "outside" is what keeps Farfield's paternalism from curdling into the kind of over-controlling panic seen in real company towns that feared losing workers to competitors — Farfield doesn't need to suppress ambition or curiosity out of fear of brain drain, because there is nowhere for anyone to drain to.

### Physical and social texture

Safford's habitat rings house genuine family neighborhoods, not barracks — multi-generational apartments, communal courtyards, small shops and crew bars that predate any given administration and carry their own local history and gossip. Administrators live in private quarters near the despun hub, the only real privacy on the station; engineers get larger shared quarters positioned for convenience; hazard crews, drone operators, and general labor live further out along the rings in smaller shared units. This stratification is real but informal — not enforced by policy so much as by decades of accumulated custom — and it coexists with a genuine, hard-won respect for skilled trades: hazard crews and maintenance techs are blue-collar in pay but high in status, because the station's survival visibly depends on them.

Daily rhythm follows an arbitrary 24-hour station clock (there is no natural day/night from outside; fluorescent panels hold a constant white-gold light in habitat sections) with three overlapping shifts tied to the refinery and gate operations at the despun hub. Food is stratified by role: administrative galleys serve genuine Earth-equivalent fare, coffee as rationed luxury; crew galleys serve competent, monotonous hydroponic-and-cultured fare, brightened only briefly when a Lugh food shipment actually arrives. Recreation — a zero-g sports court, a small library where physical books are prestige objects, crew bars — is where the real social life of the station happens, including the quiet, careful conversations of the "disillusioned idealist" strand ([Culture §1](Culture.md#1)–10.2) who haven't made peace with the system.

The station's mood by Year 92 is stable, grinding, and universally resented without being unstable: Farfield's efficiency is real (safety, reliability, adequate wages) and completely soul-crushing (no say in decisions made by a handful of administrators behind sealed doors, no possibility of leaving). Nobody expects the system to change, which is precisely what makes its sudden collapse so destabilizing ([Politics §3](Politics.md#3)).

### Education as the mechanism of consent

Safford's schools are the clearest expression of Farfield's company-town logic, because they convert coercion into what feels like meritocracy. The pitch to families is explicit: send us your child, and we will identify their natural aptitudes early, train them rigorously in whatever they're suited for, and guarantee them skilled employment by their late teens — a promise of security that Earth, with its scarce positions and millions of competing applicants, could never make.

**Tracking structure:**

- **Ages 6–10, broad exposure.** All children take shared literacy, numeracy, and basic scientific method, rotating hands-on through different station systems. Evaluation is continuous but not yet visible to families as "tracking" — a child who shows mathematical aptitude gets quietly steered toward extra coaching; a child with spatial reasoning gets more lab time; a child who's good with people gets eased toward logistics apprenticeship.
- **Ages 10–14, formal tracks.** Curricula diverge explicitly into labor-class tracks: wormhole-engineering (deep physics, mathematics, fermion theory, gate-chamber systems), hazard-crew (EVA procedure, materials science, emergency response), drone-operations (systems thinking, control theory, swarm behavior), and logistics (resource management, supply-chain mathematics). Overlap remains — safety training and basic physics are universal — but the divergence in depth and rigor is real and visible to the children living it.
- **Ages 14–18, apprenticeship.** Children work directly alongside professionals in their track, evaluated for permanent placement. A hazard-crew apprentice does supervised EVA work; an engineering apprentice takes readings in the gate chambers under a licensed engineer.

Children are **not free to choose** a track outside this system — there is no arts program, no philosophy, nothing that doesn't serve Farfield's labor needs, because Farfield has no incentive to fund anything that doesn't. But within the tracks that exist, kids are steered toward whichever one they test highest in, and the system works hard to make that steering feel like discovery rather than assignment: a child who tests well in spatial reasoning is praised, encouraged, and made to feel genuinely gifted at engineering, which makes them want to keep doing it. A child moved out of the engineering track into drone operations experiences it as finding their level, not as failure. This is what makes the system durable — it isn't received as coercion by most of the people living inside it.

Structural inequality persists underneath the meritocratic surface: administrator and engineer children grow up around the language and culture of their parents' work — dinner-table talk of gate physics, casual childhood visits to control rooms — giving them a real head start in track evaluations that has nothing to do with innate aptitude. There is likely no formal discrimination, but the advantage compounds generationally regardless.

**The curated curriculum — history, art, and politics:**

- **History** taught in all tracks is Farfield's own sanitized version: the founding of the network, the technical triumphs of expansion, the courage of first-wave colonists — never the messy realities of Earth labor history, colonist selection bias, or anything that might supply a vocabulary for grievance. The engineering track gets somewhat more genuine historical depth (understanding past failures matters for engineering judgment), but even that is Farfield-approved.
- **Art** is permitted and even encouraged as a pressure valve and a recruitment amenity, but curated: company-sanctioned music and theater, vetted scripts, nothing that asks hard questions about the system itself. Genuinely subversive or challenging art exists only informally, in crew bars and off official channels.
- **Politics** is functionally absent from the curriculum. Civics is taught as pure operational fact — how the chain of command works, why gate engineers have authority over gate operations — presented as simply how things are organized for efficiency, never as one possible arrangement among others. No labor history, no comparative governance, no ethics or philosophy asking what makes a system just. This is a deliberate absence as much as anything explicitly taught: without exposure to alternative ways of organizing a society, most residents lack even the vocabulary to articulate systemic complaint, let alone imagine an alternative.

**The generational fault line this creates:** engineering-track children, needing broader intellectual development for genuinely difficult technical work, end up with the most (if still curated) exposure to history and rigorous reasoning — occasionally enough to start asking real questions. Labor-track children, systematically kept from the same intellectual tools, feel the unfairness of the system viscerally (watching engineer children get more of everything) without the historical or philosophical vocabulary to frame it as *systemic* rather than personal. That produces a particular flavor of resentment: inarticulate, decades-deep, and — once the system that was holding it in check disappears at Year 92 — dangerous.

## 2.3 Post-Severance Gate Safeguards

Sat-39's collapse ([History §2.1](History.md#2.1)) is, on the Nova Reach side, ultimately attributed to compounding mismanagement and deferred maintenance rather than any flaw in the underlying gate design. In direct response, wormhole engineers across Nova Reach and every branch colony overhaul their techniques, regulations, and safeguards in the years that follow: tighter tuning tolerances, more frequent inspection and audit cycles, and stricter escalation procedures the moment telemetry drifts from baseline — closing exactly the kind of slow, easy-to-miss failure mode described in [§1](#1) above (a residual mass mismatch that "only surfaces when someone finally audits a gate's mass against its logged baseline and finds it's wandered").

This is now a **permanent, locked rule for the setting going forward**: any gate failure occurring after the Severance Event must be the result of deliberate sabotage, never negligence or accident. Wormhole engineers ([Culture §3](Culture.md#3)) become, in effect, the network's conscience-keepers post-collapse — the professional guardrails standing between stability and a second catastrophe, and a natural source of institutional authority and moral weight in any post-collapse story beat that touches gate operations.

### Lugh: World and Settlement

Lugh (Danu c) is a tidally-locked eyeball world, but its defining narrative feature is a **thick, cometary-delivered CO2 atmosphere** (~1.8 bar, ~95%+ CO2 with trace nitrogen and inert gases, no free oxygen) — deposited over the system's history by repeated cometary impacts rather than outgassed from the interior. Surface gravity is 0.70g (Lugh's mass was set at 0.665 M⊕ against its established 6,213.3 km radius specifically to hit this target). Both facts are deliberate choices to make Lugh read as a genuinely distinct world from Boann rather than a second underground outpost with a different name.

**Why the atmosphere matters:** unlike Boann — a purely subsurface settlement reached by deep drilling into an ice shell, dark, pressurized, claustrophobic — Lugh's atmosphere provides real radiation shielding without requiring anyone to burrow. This makes **surface settlement viable**: habitat modules, agricultural domes, and processing facilities can sit on or just above the ground rather than being buried under regolith, with actual windows and visible structures rather than tunnel entrances. People on Lugh can see their sky (however alien); people on Boann cannot.

The thick atmosphere also means Lugh has **real weather** — not Earth-style rain, but genuine circulation driven by the temperature differential between the permanent day side and the permanent night side: winds flowing from hot to cold, dust storms, thermal updrafts concentrated at the terminator itself as warm air meets cold. This gives Lugh's inhabitants an actual environmental literacy — reading wind patterns, anticipating the dry season, watching for high winds as the 22.9-day light cycle peaks — that Boann's sealed, drilled world has no equivalent for.

**Settlement architecture, and why it isn't symmetric:** Lugh's population (no hard figure established; described qualitatively as the system's primary agricultural hub) concentrates in one dominant city sited at the **leading terminator** — the point on the equator where the terminator crosses in the same direction Lugh is already moving along its orbit around Danu A. This siting is not arbitrary: launches heading outward to Safford (and onward to the rest of the network) are prograde departures, tangential to Lugh's own orbital motion, and a leading-terminator site starts every ascent already aligned with that departure vector rather than needing a costly dogleg burn to match it. Given that Lugh's entire economic purpose is *exporting* food to the network, optimizing for outbound traffic rather than splitting infrastructure between separate leading and trailing sites is the obvious call — a trailing-terminator counterpart would be isolated enough by distance and comm lag to function as an effectively separate colony, not a productive twin.

Equatorial siting more broadly (as opposed to any other latitude along the terminator) is likewise a matter of orbital mechanics, not rotation: Lugh's negligible spin velocity (a consequence of its tidal lock — rotation period equals the 22.9-day orbital period) makes any spin-assist to launch essentially irrelevant, unlike Earth. What actually matters is that the equator lies in Danu A's orbital plane, so an equatorial site's trajectories start already coplanar with the rest of the system's traffic. A settlement near either pole would sit on Lugh's rotation axis, normal to that orbital plane, and every departure or arrival from it would carry a costly out-of-plane correction that equatorial Lugh never has to pay.

Around this primary leading-terminator city, smaller settlements creep outward along the terminator line itself, north and south toward the poles — not spreading along the equator in both directions, but following the day/night boundary as it curves from equator to pole. These outlying settlements are agricultural and logistical satellites of the main city rather than independent population centers: still within the same habitable twilight band, still linked by regular transit, but progressively more marginal (thinner infrastructure, smaller populations, more exposed to the temperature swings of higher latitudes) the further they sit from the main hub. Agricultural domes and hab modules throughout — both the primary city and its satellites — use **perpetual-sunset mirror arrays** (bible-established) to redirect and concentrate the day side's light into greenhouse domes on Earth-like day/night cycles. Hab modules are a surface/shallow-subsurface hybrid: living quarters can sit above ground with real windows, while deeper levels handle resource processing and storage, insulated by regolith the way Earth basements use soil.

**Low gravity, thick air — flight becomes ordinary technology:** the combination of 0.70g and 1.8 bar CO2 (roughly 1.5× the density of Earth air at equivalent pressure, so meaningfully denser still at Lugh's actual pressure) makes powered and unpowered flight dramatically cheaper than on Earth. Aircraft that would never generate enough lift to leave Earth's ground are fully airworthy on Lugh: stubby wings, flat lifting-body shapes, mostly-envelope balloon/dirigible hybrids with minimal gondola, flight speeds as low as 20–40 km/h. Gliders and unpowered wing-suits can ride the terminator's thermal updrafts for hours. This gives Lugh a genuine **aviation culture** absent anywhere else in the network — young Lugh colonists grow up thinking three-dimensionally, reading wind and thermals the way a sailor reads current, and aircraft become the practical way to move people, equipment, and harvested crops along the terminator rather than needing the settlement to stay tightly clustered.

**Lighter-than-air travel: considered and rejected.** Lugh's 0.70g and 1.8 bar CO2 atmosphere (~2.7× Earth-surface atmospheric density) make hydrogen lift genuinely excellent in principle — roughly 2.7–2.8× the lift per cubic meter of envelope compared to an equivalent Earth hydrogen airship, and locally sourceable via water electrolysis rather than needing off-world import. The complete absence of free oxygen in Lugh's atmosphere further removes hydrogen's usual combustion hazard entirely, since there is nothing for it to burn with. Despite this, airships are ruled out as a transportation class for Lugh, at least for the current story timeframe: the terminator's thermal-driven wind regime (above) is survivable for airship operations in the open terminator band itself (comparable to a stiff trade wind), but the katabatic winds funneling off the nightside ice cap's edge run into the same range that grounds real-world Antarctic aviation — well past any airship's safe crosswind tolerance. Given that Lugh's fixed-wing fleet (Farfield A-6, AOTS/Skyreach — [Technology §1.2](Technology.md#1.2)) already covers the network's full range of atmospheric transport needs, adding a second aircraft class solely to capture airships' cargo-efficiency advantage was judged not worth the added manufacturing and maintenance burden. The idea remains on record as a plausible later development — something an enterprising post-collapse colonist might revisit once local industry matures — rather than a permanently foreclosed option.

**EVA gear and safety culture:** Lugh's ~1.8 bar, ~95%+ CO2 atmosphere is mild in pressure terms (comparable to a shallow dive, well short of anything requiring a rigid or hard-shell pressure suit) but immediately lethal to breathe — sustained exposure to ambient CO2 at this partial pressure causes hypercapnia and unconsciousness within minutes regardless of supplemental oxygen, since the hazard is what enters the lungs, not the ambient pressure on the body. The standard solution is a sealed, positive-pressure full-face respirator — mechanically closer to a firefighter's SCBA or a diver's full-face mask than to Boann's or Safford's bulky vacuum-rated EVA suits, since the regulator only needs to keep ambient gas from being pulled in on inhalation, not resist a true pressure differential. Colonists layer this respirator under ordinary weather gear (wind- and dust-proofing, goggling for regolith abrasion) rather than a full suit. Because outdoor travel between domes and satellite settlements is routine rather than exceptional, EVA safety is taught from early childhood as a basic life skill, the way swimming or road safety might be taught on Earth, and every dome exit posts a safety officer who manually inspects each person's seal and equipment before they're allowed through the airlock — a mandatory, unhurried checkpoint rather than a formality, reflecting how routinely fatal a bad seal actually is. Short-distance travel between nearby domes is handled by ground vehicles; longer trips to outlying satellite settlements default to the aircraft made practical by Lugh's low gravity and thick air (above).

**Contrast with Boann, culturally and strategically:** Boann is dark, sealed, submarine-like, defined by drilling depth and pressure vessels; Lugh is open, three-dimensional, defined by wind and light. The two settlements are genuinely interdependent (Lugh's agriculture needs Boann's water) rather than redundant, which is precisely why the alliance between them under Lugh's post-collapse leadership ([Politics §4.2](Politics.md#4.2)) is a real partnership and not a simple annexation. Militarily, Lugh's surface-exposed, visible infrastructure (domes, hab modules, mirror arrays) is considerably more vulnerable to orbital or atmospheric attack than Boann's deep-buried tunnel network — an asymmetry worth remembering for the Phase 2 military escalation ([History §3.2](History.md#3.2)), where Nova Reach's incursions would find Lugh's aboveground infrastructure a much softer target than anything dug into Boann's ice shell.

### Boann: World and Settlement

Boann (Mannannán I) is a tidally-heated subsurface ocean moon — a differentiated body with an icy shell, a thick liquid-water layer beneath that shell, and a modest silicate core. Surface gravity is 0.02g (0.196 m/s²), implying a mass of roughly 8.9 × 10²⁰ kg (~0.00015 M⊕) at a radius of 550 km, and a bulk density of ~1,275 kg/m³ — physically consistent with a real ice/rock body of this size (Saturn's actual moon Rhea, comparable radius, has a density of 1,236 kg/m³). Orbital distance from Mannannán is 350,000 km, with eccentricity 0.008 — small but nonzero, in the same range as Europa (e ≈ 0.009) and Enceladus (e ≈ 0.005), the real moons this whole settlement concept is modeled on. What actually sustains that eccentricity, rather than letting it damp out over geological time, is a **3:2 mean-motion resonance with Mannannán III**: Boann orbits Mannannán roughly every 1.50 days against III's ~2.26 days, a period ratio within half a percent of an exact 3:2 lock — the same resonance type that pairs Neptune and Pluto in the real solar system. This is what's actually behind everything else in this section: the ice-shell stress, the icequakes, the deliberately-sited tidal-heating hotspot the borehole exploits. Mannannán III itself stays an ordinary, uninhabited minor moon — sharing the resonance doesn't make it narratively significant on its own. The settlement is built around a single deep borehole, sunk through roughly 3–5 km of ice at that hotspot, opening into the subsurface ocean proper. A submerged station sits at the ocean floor above hydrothermal-vent fields; habitat cavities are carved into the ice at intervals along the shaft.

**Seafloor chemistry.** Because Boann shares Danu A's high-Mg/Si stellar composition with Lugh (above), its rocky core is likewise forsterite-rich rather than solar-typical — and forsterite is close to the purest possible ultramafic feedstock for **serpentinization**, the real hydrothermal process (well-documented at Earth's own deep-sea vent systems, and the leading theorized energy source for Europa's and Enceladus's own subsurface oceans) where ultramafic rock reacts with seawater to release free hydrogen. This makes Boann's seafloor a genuinely favorable site for vigorous serpentinization chemistry — plausibly richer hydrogen production and more vigorous hydrothermal vent activity than an Earth-composition seafloor would produce, a purely abiotic geochemical process throughout (no native or introduced life anywhere in the setting — [Cosmology §1.1](Cosmology.md#1.1)). This was a deliberate factor in setting the high-Mg/Si stellar composition (above): the choice pays off for Lugh's visible surface geology and for Boann's seafloor hydrogen-production chemistry simultaneously, rather than trading one off against the other.

**Excavation is mechanical, not thermal, and it pays for itself.** Melting a shaft this size would cost on the order of 6,000+ GJ and creates an unstable standing-water problem in near-vacuum surface conditions (flash-boiling at the top, refreezing lower down). Mechanical cutting — a rotary head fracturing ice at the working face, hauled to surface by cable hoist — is roughly two orders of magnitude cheaper: for a representative 2 m-diameter, 4 km shaft (~12,500 m³), fracture energy runs ~63 GJ and lift energy against Boann's 0.02g gravity is a negligible ~4.5 GJ, for a total of roughly 67 GJ. Recovered ice is hauled to the surface and melted in controlled tanks, not left standing in the shaft. This makes habitat expansion nearly self-funding: every cubic meter of new chamber space produces a cubic meter of exactly the resource the colony exists to export — excavation *is* the harvest. The one exception is the final few meters of breakthrough into the ocean itself, deliberately melted under a sealed docking collar rather than mechanically punched through, to engineer a controlled transition instead of risking a blowout.

**0.02g is deep into ISS-microgravity territory** — well below the threshold for maintaining human bone density and muscle mass over anything longer than months — and a stationary borehole has no mechanism for artificial gravity the way Safford's rotating rings do. Farfield's own official medical guidance treats this as rotational-only ([§Deck Pitch and the Healthy Gravity Range](#deck-pitch-and-the-healthy-gravity-range) above): tours of months to a couple of years, then mandatory rotation out to recover. In practice, this is not what happens. A meaningful share of Boann's population — the **Settled Crew** — never rotates out at all, some by genuine choice and some because Farfield frames long-term postings as elite, low-stress advancement rather than disclosing the point of no return ([Culture, Boann subsection](Culture.md#3)). Past a real, cumulative physiological threshold, leaving stops being medically simple regardless of what anyone officially intends. Boann is consequently a genuinely multi-generational settlement, not the purely rotational outpost Farfield's paperwork describes — children are born and raised there, adapted to 0.02g from birth, with no meaningful prospect of ever living at a higher gravity. This is the real reason Boann reads as the network's weakest, least independent settlement ([Politics §4.1](Politics.md#4.1)): its multi-generational population exists, but it exists as a trap rather than as the kind of freely-chosen rootedness that gives Lugh its leverage.

**Physiological effects and the point of no return.** Real spaceflight medicine grounds this rather than inventing it. Bone density loss and muscle atrophy at near-zero effective load are well-documented ISS effects; a body raised at 0.02g from birth never builds the load-bearing bone architecture (Wolff's Law) or cardiovascular pumping strength that any real gravity gradient assumes, rather than losing a baseline it once had. For adults, the point of no return is defined as roughly **two years of cumulative exposure** — a real, internally-tracked Farfield threshold nobody discloses in those terms (see [Culture, Boann subsection](Culture.md#3)). Past that point, or for anyone born and raised on Boann, a transfer to Safford's 0.5g is survivable only with serious, sustained medical support (below) and never fully reversible.

**Body composition runs soft, not lean.** Two real effects compound rather than cancel out: locomotion at 0.02g costs a small fraction of the energy Earth-normal walking does, and atrophied muscle lowers resting metabolic rate — both push total energy expenditure down. With no rationing regime restricting intake (food is closed-loop and functional, [below], but not scarce), the honest physiological default is **lower muscle tone paired with higher body fat**, not the lean, elongated "spacer" look genre convention might suggest. Boannites read as softer and somewhat bloated next to a Safford-born colonist, an effect compounded by real, well-documented cephalad fluid shift (fluid redistributing toward the head and torso without gravity to pull it downward). Some degree of additional stature from unloaded spinal growth during childhood is physiologically plausible but not established fact — flagged here as speculative extrapolation, not locked physics, consistent with this bible's general methodology.

**Mitigation technology is real, partial, and unequally distributed.** The **Boann Centrifuge Complex** is a large-radius, low-rpm surface training facility, purpose-built to prepare Settled Crew members for eventual reassignment to higher gravity — cheap and comfortable to build precisely because Boann's own 0.02g means the structure barely has to fight its own weight, and because there's almost no background gravity vector to cross-couple against the spin (the effect that makes real short-arm centrifuges disorienting on Earth). Alongside it, compact **sleep pods** provide passive nightly loading — a real, studied countermeasure concept (short-radius centrifugation during bed-rest analogs measurably slows bone and muscle loss versus no loading at all) — genuinely extending how long a worker can remain on Boann before crossing the two-year threshold, without ever eliminating it, since two-thirds of every day is still spent unloaded regardless. Sleep pods are available to any worker, but cost money — a real, working protection gated behind a paycheck, so newer and poorer workers (often the ones least aware of the threshold in the first place) tend to cross it sooner. **2104 bone-repair medicine** (bone morphogenetic proteins, 3D-bioprinted patient-matched scaffolds, localized ultrasound/electrical stimulation, targeted gene therapy, and mechanotransduction devices that fake the mechanical-loading signal bone needs to remodel correctly) treats an individual fracture well — but every one of these interventions is local to the repair site, not systemic, so none of it reverses the underlying whole-skeleton underloading. A well-healed Boannite fracture and a permanently low-density Boannite skeleton are not in tension; both are true at once.

**Hazards and culture.** Tidal flexing produces ongoing ice-shell stress (icequakes, shaft fatigue) as a slow, ever-present background hazard; the submerged station and lower shaft carry genuine pressure-differential risk, closer to submarine hazards than anything Lugh or Safford deals with. There is no daylight cycle of any kind — not even Lugh's slow twilight — only the constant artificial day of electric light and the psychological weight of kilometers of ice overhead. Real-world analogues (submarine crews, Antarctic winter-over staff) suggest a tight-knit, technically rigorous work culture with dark humor as a coping mechanism — see [Culture, Boann subsection](Culture.md#3) for the fuller cultural treatment ("Settling," gallows humor, and the moon's quasi-devotional personification). Where Lugh's culture centers on cultivation, patience, and generational rootedness, Boann's centers on competence and endurance under constraint — a rig crew's ethic, not a farmer's. This, combined with Boann's near-instant comm coupling to Safford ([§5](#5)), leaves it structurally closer to Enlil's tight corporate subordination than to Lugh's independence, and is the underlying reason it produces no rival leader after the collapse ([Politics §4.1](Politics.md#4.1)), instead being absorbed into Lugh's authority.

**Mobility: no walking, ever.** At 0.02g there is no meaningful "down." Movement through both the shaft's habitat cavities and the submerged station is handhold- and guide-cable propulsion, ISS-style, not walking — floors as a single privileged surface mostly don't exist as a design concept, since there's little reason to favor one orientation over the others. Long-tenure workers move with a fluency that reads as unnervingly efficient to a station-standard (0.5g) visitor, a physical tell marking real time served on Boann.

**Fluids, hygiene, and atmosphere.** Nothing gravity-drains at 0.02g. Drinking water, waste, and process water from the vent operations are all fully enclosed, pressurized, forced-flow systems — open basins or drains don't function. Showers are sealed, suction-based reclaim pods rather than spray-and-drain fixtures. Ventilation is forced throughout, not convective, which makes it load-bearing for basic breathability rather than a comfort feature; fire behaves differently and, absent gravity's usual visible warning cues (rising smoke, a flame that leans and flickers), is arguably a more institutionally serious hazard here than on Lugh or at Safford.

**Food: closed-loop culture, vent-fed.** Sealed photobioreactor tanks — algae or engineered microbial protein culture, artificially lit — draw on vent-adjacent water chemistry rather than imported nutrient stock, plausibly using the free hydrogen and mineral-rich fluid the serpentinization process (above) already produces as feedstock. This makes food production symbiotic with the extraction economy rather than a separate system, and supplements rather than replaces shipped staples from Lugh. Daily food is mostly processed protein/algae product — functional, not enjoyable — and a resupply shipment of "real" produce from Lugh is a genuine occasion, the same emotional beat real submarine and Antarctic-station crews report around fresh food.

**Power.** A stationary Trelium fusion reactor, network-standard fuel cycle ([Technology §1.1](Technology.md#1.1)) but fixed rather than mounted for thrust — no solar is possible under kilometers of ice. Fuel is imported through the gate network from Goibniu, not locally extracted from Mannannán — Nova Reach's Trelium monopoly ([Economy §2](Economy.md#2)) is a real, established source of structural leverage network-wide, and Mannannán's own gas giant is never developed as an alternative source, the same way Obatala's gas giant is judged not worth the infrastructure ([Cosmology §5.4](Cosmology.md#5.4)). This makes Boann's reactor, and by extension its entire population, directly dependent on Nova Reach's gate-network supply chain in a way that compounds its already-established structural weakness ([Politics §4.1](Politics.md#4.1)) — a second, independent point of leverage alongside its near-instant comm coupling to Safford.

**The workforce: who goes, and the shape of a tour.** Recruitment reads closer to offshore-rig or Antarctic winter-over hiring than colonization: specialized technical roles (ice engineers, submersible pilots, vent-extraction crew, station maintenance), substantial hazard pay, and a population skewing toward earlier-career workers trading a bounded physiological cost for compensation or advancement rather than committing to it as a lifelong vocation. Nobody brings family — whatever family a worker has lives elsewhere, most plausibly Lugh or Safford, and home life is conducted entirely in the gaps between tours. A tour has a consistent shape: an **arrival adaptation period** (disorientation and relearning locomotion even for experienced hands); **monitored decline** during the tour itself, with mandatory resistance exercise and the same bone/muscle-preserving pharmaceutical and gene-therapy countermeasures used against radiation damage elsewhere in the network, tracked against hard medical thresholds; a **tour-end trigger**, either the standard months-to-couple-years cap or an early pull if a worker's deconditioning outpaces the norm; and a **mandatory recovery period** at a higher-gravity settlement afterward before requalification for further duty.

**Cumulative exposure.** Recovery plausibly doesn't fully reverse each tour's cost, implying a real lifetime limit on how many tours a given worker can safely serve — and, with it, a natural career ceiling and a genuine personal stake (a veteran weighing "one more tour" against a real, embodied cost) that experienced hands eventually age out of field work and into training or administrative roles. Whether this is a hard, numeric lifetime cap or case-by-case medical judgment is not yet decided ([Open Questions](Open_Questions.md)).

**Reproductive policy.** Given total unknowns around low-g fetal development and the fact that nobody is meant to stay long-term regardless, pregnancy triggers immediate rotation out — a straightforward medical protocol in-universe, not a live ethical debate, consistent with how seriously the tour-cap system already treats bone/muscle thresholds.

**Social texture.** Small, tight, technically competent crews, with the dark humor already noted above — but with everyone there temporary by design, relationships carry a built-in horizon. This is a different social fabric from Lugh's rootedness or even Safford's larger rotational corporate crew: closer to a deployment than a posting.

### Safford Station (Sat-48 Obatala)
 
Sited as a minor moon co-orbital with Olokun — a Phobos/Deimos-style small body rather than the gas-giant-moon pattern used at Nova Reach and Danu, and a deliberate departure from the station's original asteroid-belt siting. This puts Safford in near-instant comm contact with Olokun (under 10 seconds one-way, [§5](#5)) but genuinely far from Yemoja — roughly 9 AU of separation at Olokun's distance, versus the old belt site's ~2.5 AU. See [Cosmology §5.2](Cosmology.md#5.2)'s Olokun subsection and [Politics §4.5](Politics.md#4.5)/[Politics §5.1](Politics.md#5.1) for how this reversed comm-delay relationship (Olokun now tightly coupled, Yemoja now the isolated world) reshapes Obatala's governance. Habitat: a short arc-module on the network-standard 111.8 m/2.000 rpm/0.5g design ([§2.1](#2.1)), sized to Safford Obatala's 90-person operational crew — a 39.7 m arc (20.3°), balanced by the standard four-mass counterweight system. Crew are Nova Reach personnel on rotational tours, not counted toward Obatala's own population.
### Olokun: World and Settlement

Olokun is a liquid methane/ethane ocean world at 0.8g — deliberately set close to Earth-normal gravity so its settlement, unlike Boann's, supports genuine multi-generational habitation. The dense, cold atmosphere (methane/ethane-dominant, ~2–5 bar) is what actually keeps the ocean liquid at this distance, trapping enough heat to hold surface temperature near 90 K despite Olokun sitting well past the system's nominal habitable zone. The moderate 22° axial tilt combined with ~0.15–0.20 orbital eccentricity produces genuinely complex seasons: a roughly 27–30 year orbital period means each seasonal phase lasts years, and the eccentricity means some years are hotter or colder than the tilt alone would predict — colonists plan around multi-year cycles rather than annual ones, and a "harsh winter" may not arrive on a perfectly predictable schedule.

**Economic role: hydrocarbon extraction first, agriculture second.** Olokun was originally settled to harvest liquid methane and ethane directly from the ocean — fractional distillation and cryogenic separation exploiting the different boiling points of the two compounds, powered by geothermal taps into the warmer crust below the frozen-solid permafrost layer. Settlement architecture mixes anchored harvesting platforms near active extraction zones with underground coastal bases for habitation, processing, and shelter from the long seasonal extremes. EVA work requires full pressure suits rated for the cold and the dense atmosphere; a breach is lethal in minutes.

**Post-collapse agricultural buildout.** Before the collapse, a small hydroponics operation supplemented Lugh's food shipments: ice imported from the system's 2–4 AU asteroid belt (a multi-month haul, given the distance) supplied water, and a modest reactor-fed sunlamp array made limited indoor growing possible — Olokun's actual sunlight, at this distance, is negligible (roughly 7–9 W/m², versus Earth's ~1,360) and useless for agriculture on its own. After Year 92, Nova Reach invests heavily in scaling this up specifically to reduce dependency on an increasingly independent Danu: imported ice tonnage increases, the power grid expands to support much larger sunlamp-lit hydroponic domes, and food self-sufficiency becomes a strategic priority rather than a supplementary pilot project. Because Obatala f's Trelium yield is too poor to be worth developing (above), the expanded reactors remain fueled by shipments from Nova Reach — meaning Olokun's post-collapse agricultural independence from Danu is bought at the cost of deeper dependency on Nova Reach for fuel, hardware, and technical staff.

**Why this makes Olokun the controlled node, not Yemoja.** Nova Reach's investment here isn't just capital, it's ownership: the reactors, the ice-shipping contracts, and the hydroponic domes are built and held as direct corporate assets, developed from scratch as a strategic post-collapse program rather than growing organically from local self-sufficiency the way Lugh's agriculture did. Paired with Safford's near-instant comm coupling to Olokun, this makes Olokun the one Obatala settlement Nova Reach can — and does — govern in something close to real time, structurally similar to Enlil's tight subordination ([Politics §4.4](Politics.md#4.4)) rather than Lugh's independence. See [Politics §4.5](Politics.md#4.5) and [Politics §5.1](Politics.md#5.1) for the resulting governance picture, where Yemoja (now genuinely distant from Safford) develops the real local autonomy instead.
### Safford Station (Sat-48 Enlil)
 
Sited on a rubble-pile remnant beyond Ereshkigal, at roughly 0.05 AU (versus Ereshkigal's ~0.01–0.02 AU) — a deliberately close placement, only ~0.035 AU of separation. Of the three branch stations, this is the only one built directly into the wreckage of a dead system rather than docked to an intact planet or moon, and it sits close enough to Ereshkigal that station and outpost are functionally neighbors (see [Travel §1](Travel.md#1) and [Technology §1.3](Technology.md#1.3)). Because the host body is a loose rubble pile rather than a solid moon or asteroid, both the gate anchor and habitat anchor are built as wide-footprint pier "rafts" rather than a few deep piers ([§2.1](#2.1)). Habitat: a short arc-module on the network-standard 111.8 m/2.000 rpm/0.5g design ([§2.1](#2.1)), sized to Safford Enlil's 50-person operational crew — the smallest of the three branch wings, a 22.0 m arc (11.3°), balanced by the standard four-mass counterweight system. Crew are Nova Reach personnel on rotational tours, not counted toward Ereshkigal's population ([Cosmology §6.1](Cosmology.md#6.1) above).

## 4 Target Mass by Wormhole
 
Since a wormhole's two mouths always share one mass ([Cosmology §2](Cosmology.md#2)) — inflation raises both ends together, and only uncorrected transit imbalance can pull them apart — target mass is set once per wormhole, not once per station. The evacuation-time floor is ~3,500 t (roughly an hour's warning in a worst-case collapse); the cost-driven ceiling is ~50,000 t. Each of the network's four wormholes is targeted within that range according to its typical throughput and, for Sat-39, its historical burden:
 
| Wormhole | Target mass | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enlil | ~5,000 t | Smallest population (2,000–4,000), single low-throughput outpost; sits near the evacuation-time floor |
| Obatala | ~9,000–10,000 t | Moderate two-settlement economy; pre-collapse agriculture still underdeveloped relative to Danu |
| Danu | ~18,000–20,000 t | Lugh is the network's primary agricultural exporter feeding the Nova Reach hub — highest sustained branch throughput |
| Sat-39 (Sol ↔ Nova Reach) | ~30,000 t | Sole colonization artery for roughly the first two to three decades (see [timeline.md](./timeline.md)) — heaviest sustained one-way historical burden in the network |
 
**Working theory of the collapse (unconfirmed in-universe):** Sat-39's mass target was the largest in the network, but its imbalance was also proportionally the worst — for roughly the first quarter of Nova Reach's history, nearly all traffic ran Sol-to-colony, with nothing of comparable value flowing back until local Trelium production and seed-harvesting existed to export. A large buffer bought decades rather than immunity, consistent with a collapse that took 92 years to arrive rather than one that struck early. Farfield's established cost-cutting culture ([Politics §3](Politics.md#3)) gives a plausible, mundane reason nobody prioritized balancing return shipments to Earth over that period. This remains a candidate explanation, not a settled one — nothing in-universe confirms it, and the failure is still experienced by every character as sudden and uncaused.

# 5. Communication Delays
 
Gate-to-gate communication is near-instant (fiber-optic threading, [Cosmology §2](Cosmology.md#2)). In-system communication, however, is limited by the speed of light, and — because planets and stations orbit their star independently — the delay between any two bodies varies continuously between a minimum (aligned on the same side of the star) and a maximum (on opposite sides), as their orbital phases drift in and out of alignment.
 
| Route | Separation range | One-way delay | Round-trip delay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Danu: Safford (Mannannán, 6 AU) ↔ Lugh (0.15 AU) | 5.85–6.15 AU | 48.7–51.2 min | 1.62–1.70 hr |
| Danu: Safford ↔ Boann (co-orbital moon of Mannannán) | moon-system scale, not heliocentric | under ~7 sec | under ~15 sec |
| Obatala: Safford (Olokun moon, ~9.5 AU) ↔ Yemoja (0.45 AU) | 7.4–11.6 AU (varies with Olokun's eccentric orbit, [Cosmology §5.2](Cosmology.md#5.2)) | 61.5–96.5 min | 2.05–3.22 hr |
| Obatala: Safford ↔ Olokun (co-orbital minor moon, Phobos/Deimos-style, [Cosmology §5.2](Cosmology.md#5.2)) | moon-system scale, not heliocentric | under ~10 sec | under ~15 sec |
| Enlil: Safford (rubble pile, 0.05 AU) ↔ Ereshkigal (0.015 AU) | 0.035–0.065 AU | 17.5–32.4 sec | 34.9–64.9 sec |
 
*Nova Reach has no equivalent entry: its population lives on the two stations themselves rather than on a planet, so there is no station-to-settlement comm lag internal to the system.*

### 5.1 Post-Collapse Fiber Degradation: A Second, Quieter Severance

The near-instant gate-to-gate threading above is accurate for Year 92 and stays accurate for some time after, but it is not a permanent fixture. Real long-haul fiber-optic transmission depends on periodic amplification, and the standard real-world method — erbium-doped fiber amplifiers — depends on exactly the rare-earth dopant supply chain the network never built local extraction or refining capacity for ([Technology §2.1](Technology.md#2.1)). Pre-collapse, this was invisible: amplifier modules were routine consumables, resupplied from Earth like anything else. Severance strands the network with whatever amplifier stock existed at Year 92, with no path to more — a second, quieter Severance running in parallel with the electronics reconstruction arc ([Technology §5.1](Technology.md#5.1)), on its own independent timeline.

**Gradual, not catastrophic.** Bandwidth and reliability decline over decades as amplifier segments fail and aren't replaced, not a single cutoff. Noticeable degradation plausibly begins a decade or two post-collapse, becoming a serious strategic concern by Years 150–200 — running roughly alongside, but mechanically independent of, the locked electronics eras: reaching indigenous ICs ([Technology §5.1](Technology.md#5.1)) is a fabrication achievement and does nothing to fix a materials-refining problem.

**Rationing follows politics, not physics.** Whichever authority controls a given hub prioritizes which links stay full-bandwidth (command/coordination traffic) and which are allowed to degrade (routine civilian data) — a real lever of inter-faction control, and a plausible flashpoint for a colony that finds itself deprioritized.

**Cannibalization, consistent with the pattern already established for the Frozen Archive** ([Technology §5.2](Technology.md#5.2)): lower-priority routes get harvested for amplifier modules to keep critical routes alive, the same "keep the important thing alive by eating the unimportant thing" logic playing out a second time in a different system.

**Not something anyone alive at Year 92 will see fixed.** Reversing this requires local rare-earth extraction and refining — a separate, still-open long-horizon possibility ([Open Questions](Open_Questions.md)), not a near-term resolution.
